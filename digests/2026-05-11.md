## Worth a Deeper Look

**Naked mole rat longevity gene transfer: from mice to human trials**

The University of Rochester's successful transfer of the naked mole rat HAS2 gene into mice — resulting in a 4.4% lifespan extension and substantially improved healthspan — represents more than a scientific curiosity. It's a proof-of-principle that longevity mechanisms evolved in one mammal can be exported to another, and the team is now actively pursuing human applications.

Naked mole rats are evolutionary outliers: they live up to 41 years (nearly ten times the lifespan of similar-sized rodents), rarely develop cancer, and show unusual resistance to neurodegeneration, cardiovascular disease, and age-related inflammation. The Rochester team, led by Vera Gorbunova and Andrei Seluanov, identified high molecular weight hyaluronic acid (HMW-HA) as a key mechanism. Naked mole rats produce roughly ten times more HMW-HA than humans, and when the team removed it from naked mole rat cells, those cells became significantly more likely to form tumors.

The genetically modified mice carrying the naked mole rat HAS2 gene showed higher levels of hyaluronan in multiple tissues, better protection against spontaneous tumors and chemically induced skin cancer, reduced inflammation across tissues as they aged, and better gut health. The 4.4% median lifespan increase is modest in absolute terms, but the broader significance is that a specific longevity adaptation was successfully moved across species without requiring full-organism cloning or embryonic manipulation.

The researchers are now pursuing two routes toward human benefit: slowing the breakdown of HMW-HA that naturally occurs in the body, or enhancing HMW-HA synthesis. Gorbunova confirmed they have already identified molecules that slow down hyaluronan degradation and are testing them in pre-clinical trials. The work was originally published in Nature in 2023, but resurfaced in May 2026 coverage as the team advances closer to translational applications. The next challenge is determining whether these mechanisms can be safely adapted to improve human healthspan without unintended consequences — a question that will require careful dose-finding studies and long-term safety monitoring before reaching clinical use.

[ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/05/260510030948.htm) | [University of Rochester](https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/gene-transfer-hmw-ha-naked-mole-rats-extends-mice-lifespan-565032/)

**Columbia's BISC brain chip: semiconductor manufacturing meets neuroscience**

Columbia's Biological Interface System to Cortex (BISC) represents an architectural shift in brain-computer interfaces: rather than building outward from bulky electronics housed in skull-mounted canisters, the entire system is condensed onto a single 50-micrometer-thick silicon chip that slides into the subdural space between the brain and skull "like a piece of wet tissue paper."

The engineering leap is substantial. BISC integrates 65,536 electrodes, 1,024 simultaneous recording channels, and 16,384 stimulation channels onto one complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) chip with a total volume of approximately 3 mm³. The chip includes a radio transceiver, wireless powering circuit, digital control, power management, data conversion, and analog signal processing — all on a single piece of silicon manufactured using TSMC's 0.13-μm Bipolar-CMOS-DMOS process. The battery-powered relay station powers and communicates with the implant via a custom ultrawideband radio link that achieves 100 Mbps data bandwidth, which then connects to external computers via 802.11 WiFi.

This density is only possible because the team leveraged semiconductor industry manufacturing techniques developed for consumer electronics. Ken Shepard, the Lau Family Professor of Electrical Engineering at Columbia who led the project, noted that "semiconductor technology has made this possible, allowing the computing power of room-sized computers to now fit in your pocket. We are now doing the same for medical implantables, allowing complex electronics to exist in the body while taking up almost no space."

Preclinical studies in pigs and nonhuman primates demonstrated chronic, reliable recordings for weeks to months from motor, sensory, and visual cortices. The team recently received NIH funding to explore BISC for drug-resistant epilepsy, and short-term intraoperative recordings are already underway in human patients during neurosurgery. The minimally invasive form factor — the chip can be inserted through a small skull opening without brain-penetrating electrodes or wires tethering it to the skull — is designed to minimize tissue reactivity and signal degradation over time.

To accelerate commercial translation, the Columbia and Stanford teams launched Kampto Neurotech, a spin-off company founded by Columbia electrical engineering alumnus Dr. Nanyu Zeng, one of the project's lead engineers. The company is producing research-ready versions of the chip and raising funds to advance the system toward human use. If BISC proves safe and effective in clinical trials, its scalable manufacturing and small form factor could enable widespread deployment for epilepsy management, motor and speech restoration in paralysis patients, and potentially visual prosthetics for blindness — applications that have long been technically feasible but commercially impractical due to the size, cost, and surgical complexity of first-generation BCI systems.
