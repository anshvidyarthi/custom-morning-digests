**Naked mole rat longevity gene successfully transferred to mice** — 
University of Rochester scientists transferred a longevity gene (Has2 encoding high-molecular-weight hyaluronan) from naked mole rats into mice, resulting in healthier animals that lived longer, with the modified mice showing increased median lifespan of about 4.4%
. 
The mice carrying the gene stayed healthier overall, had less inflammation in multiple tissues as they aged, and maintained better gut health, with chronic inflammation reduction being especially important
. [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/05/260510030948.htm)

## Worth a Deeper Look

**United Therapeutics wins FDA clearance for first xenoheart clinical trial — what it means for transplantation**

United Therapeutics' May 15 announcement that the FDA has cleared the EXPRESS clinical trial for its UHeart xenotransplant marks a watershed moment in solid-organ xenotransplantation. While the company and others have performed compassionate-use xenotransplants in individual patients over the past three years, this is the first trial designed to generate data supporting a Biologics License Application — the regulatory pathway to commercial availability.

The UHeart is derived from a pig with 10 gene edits: six added human genes to facilitate immunological acceptance (reducing hyperacute rejection mediated by pre-formed antibodies) and four knocked-out porcine genes to reduce rejection risk and moderate organ growth. This is a more complex genetic modification than the single-gene-edited organs (removing alpha-gal alone) used in earlier compassionate-use cases, reflecting lessons learned from years of preclinical work.

The trial design is cautious and appropriate for a first-in-human study. The initial cohort will enroll up to two participants, with United Therapeutics providing the FDA with safety and efficacy data from the first recipient before enrolling the second. Following FDA review of data from both initial transplants, the study may expand to support a BLA submission. This staged approach allows regulators to assess real-world safety signals before exposing additional patients to risk.

The significance extends beyond hearts. United Therapeutics has three active xenotransplant programs: UHeart (10-gene-edited pig hearts), UKidney (10-gene-edited pig kidneys), and UThymoKidney (single-gene-edited kidney plus thymus). The UKidney trial (EXPAND) began enrolling patients in November 2025 and has already transplanted its first patient at NYU Langone Health. The company operates a designated pathogen-free facility in Stewartville, Minnesota, with capacity for approximately 125 organs per year, and is building additional capacity.

The bottleneck is no longer scientific proof-of-concept — NYU Langone, University of Maryland, and University of Alabama Birmingham have collectively performed dozens of pig-to-human transplants in compassionate-use and deceased-donor research settings, with organ function lasting days to months. The bottleneck is now regulatory validation: demonstrating reproducible safety and efficacy across multiple patients in controlled trial settings, under standardized immunosuppression protocols, with systematic data collection.

If United Therapeutics' trials succeed, xenotransplantation could begin addressing the organ shortage within 3-5 years. More than 100,000 Americans are currently on the transplant waiting list, with 17 dying each day before receiving an organ. Xenotransplantation would not replace human organ donation — human organs will remain the gold standard where available — but could provide a bridge for patients who would otherwise die waiting, or for whom human transplant is not an option due to sensitization (high levels of anti-human antibodies).

The ethical, logistical, and economic questions remain unresolved: How will society decide who receives xenotransplants? How will insurance cover experimental organs that cost hundreds of thousands of dollars per procedure? What long-term monitoring is required for patients carrying porcine DNA? But the FDA's clearance to proceed signals that the scientific and regulatory infrastructure is mature enough to begin answering those questions through controlled human trials.
