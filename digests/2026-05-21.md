## 🧬 Biotech / Longevity / Neurotech

**New senolytic class clears aged cells in humans without toxicity** — 
Researchers at the Mayo Clinic published results from a first-in-human trial of a novel senolytic compound, SSK1, which successfully cleared senescent cells from blood and tissue samples in 18 participants aged 65-80 with no serious adverse events. Unlike earlier senolytics like dasatinib+quercetin, SSK1 targets the BCL-xL pathway with 40-fold greater specificity, reducing off-target effects on platelets. The trial showed measurable reductions in p16INK4a-positive cells and inflammatory cytokines after a single dose, with effects lasting at least 90 days. [Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-03498-1)

**Neuralink achieves 2,048-electrode recording stability in three patients** — 
Neuralink reported six-month follow-up data on its Link 2.5 implant showing stable recording from 2,048 electrodes across motor cortex in three paralyzed participants, with average signal quality remaining above 85% of baseline and no thread retraction events. The device enabled participants to control computers, tablets, and robotic arms with latencies under 30 milliseconds, representing the highest-channel-count stable BCI deployment in humans to date. [Neuralink Blog](https://neuralink.com/blog/link-2-5-six-month-update/)

**FDA grants accelerated approval to CRISPR therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy** — 
The FDA approved Sarepta Therapeutics' SRP-9003 under accelerated approval pathway for ambulatory Duchenne muscular dystrophy patients aged 4-7, based on Phase 2 data showing sustained micro-dystrophin expression and improved six-minute walk distance at 48 weeks. This marks the first gene-editing therapy approved for DMD and the third CRISPR-based drug to reach the U.S. market after sickle cell and beta-thalassemia treatments. Confirmatory Phase 3 results are expected in Q4 2026. [FDA Press Release](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-gene-editing-therapy-duchenne-muscular-dystrophy)

**Precision Neuroscience's Layer 7 Cortical Interface achieves 16,384 channels in first surgery** — 
Precision Neuroscience successfully implanted its Layer 7 system—a thin-film electrode array with 16,384 recording channels—on the cortical surface of a paralyzed patient at Mount Sinai Hospital. The subdural implant covers 8 cm² of motor and sensory cortex and was placed in a single four-hour procedure without penetrating brain tissue. Early data shows distinct neural signatures for intended finger movements within 72 hours post-surgery. [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/precision-neuroscience-layer-7-16k-channels)

**Calico publishes largest longevity GWAS identifying 19 new lifespan-associated loci** — 
Calico, in collaboration with AncestryDNA, published a genome-wide association study of 1.2 million individuals identifying 19 novel genetic loci associated with human lifespan. The study found that cumulative genetic effects account for roughly 12% of lifespan variation, with newly identified variants linked to DNA repair, mitochondrial function, and inflammatory pathways. Several variants showed sex-specific effects, particularly in genes related to immune regulation in women. [Nature Aging](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-026-00521-4)

**Altos Labs reports partial cellular reprogramming reverses liver fibrosis in mice** — 
Altos Labs published preclinical data showing that transient expression of Yamanaka factors (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4) for 7 days reversed established liver fibrosis in aged mice without inducing teratomas or loss of cellular identity. Treated mice showed 60% reduction in collagen deposition and restoration of hepatic function markers to levels comparable to young animals. The study provides proof-of-concept that partial reprogramming can reverse tissue-level pathology, not just epigenetic age. [Cell](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(26)00543-2)

## Worth a deeper look

**OpenAI's GPT-5.9 test-time search: inference becomes training**

GPT-5.9's "test-time search" capability represents a fundamental architectural shift in how frontier models allocate compute. Rather than generating a single autoregressive sequence, the model explores multiple solution paths during inference using a variant of Monte Carlo tree search guided by learned value functions. For complex tasks—particularly coding problems with verifiable correctness—this allows the model to allocate dramatically more compute to harder problems while using minimal resources for straightforward queries.

The approach builds on ideas from AlphaGo and AlphaZero, where search over discrete action spaces dramatically improved performance beyond what raw pattern recognition could achieve. In GPT-5.9, each "node" in the search tree represents a partial solution (e.g., partially implemented function), and the value function estimates probability of reaching a correct solution from that state. The model explores high-value branches more deeply, backtracking when solutions fail unit tests or type checking.

On SWE-Bench Verified, this pushed accuracy from 92.1% (GPT-5.8) to 97.3%, with the remaining failures concentrated in problems requiring multi-file refactors or complex state management. OpenAI reports the median problem uses 8 seconds of search; the hardest 10% use up to 5 minutes. Pricing reflects this: $6/million tokens for input and $30/million for output, plus $0.50 per search minute—making a 5-minute search cost roughly $2.50 in addition to token charges.

The architectural implication is profound: inference is no longer a fixed-cost operation but a budget allocation problem. For production systems, this means developers must decide how much "thinking time" to allocate per query, balancing accuracy against cost and latency. OpenAI's API allows setting search budgets in 30-second increments, with the model automatically terminating when it reaches high confidence or exhausts the budget.

[OpenAI Blog](https://openai.com/index/gpt-5-9-test-time-search)

**Claude Desktop 2.0: persistent context finally works**

Anthropic's Claude Desktop 2.0 solves a problem that has plagued AI coding assistants since their inception: context continuity across sessions. Previous versions required developers to manually re-establish project state every time they opened the IDE, re-uploading documentation, explaining architectural decisions, and reminding the model of conventions. Desktop 2.0 introduces persistent workspace memory that survives restarts, tracking active threads, recent decisions, file-edit history, and project-specific instructions.

Under the hood, Desktop 2.0 maintains a local vector database of workspace interactions, updated incrementally as you work. When you start a new conversation, Claude automatically retrieves relevant context from past sessions—recent file edits, unresolved threads, coding conventions extracted from previous reviews—and injects it into the prompt without manual copy-paste. The system uses a lightweight embedding model to index interactions in real-time with minimal performance overhead.

The multi-app orchestration feature extends this persistence across tool boundaries. If Claude is debugging a failing test in Terminal and needs to check the implementation in VS Code, it can switch contexts automatically while maintaining the thread of reasoning. The system tracks which tools hold relevant state and routes queries accordingly, eliminating the need for developers to manually coordinate between apps.

"Project modes" let teams codify these patterns. A team can define a "backend API review" mode that automatically loads API design guidelines, runs linting and type checks, and posts summaries to Slack—all without manual configuration. Modes are stored as JSON files in the repo, making them versionable and shareable across teams.

The update represents a shift from stateless chat interactions to stateful development environments where the AI maintains genuine continuity. For teams that have struggled with context repetition fatigue, this should meaningfully reduce overhead.
