**Guardant Health wins FDA approval for largest liquid biopsy panel** — 
Guardant Health announced that the FDA has approved Guardant360 Liquid CDx, advancing blood-based comprehensive genomic testing by integrating genomic and epigenomic insights and helping clinicians make better-informed treatment selection decisions for patients with advanced cancer. Guardant360 Liquid CDx is the largest FDA-approved liquid biopsy panel.
 The approval expands the utility of liquid biopsy beyond DNA sequencing to include epigenetic markers that may inform treatment resistance and response.

**HAYA Therapeutics doses first cohort in cellular reprogramming trial for cardiac fibrosis** — 
HAYA Therapeutics announced that it has dosed the first cohort in its Phase 1 trial of HTX-001, an investigational cellular reprogramming therapy for cardiac fibrosis.
 The approach targets fibroblast cells in the heart to reverse pathological remodeling, representing a novel mechanism distinct from traditional heart failure therapies that focus on hemodynamics or neurohormonal blockade.

**Johns Hopkins creates general-purpose antibody for ADC manufacturing** — 
Johns Hopkins University scientists created a general-purpose antibody with six mutations in its Fc region, offering a customizable system to ease manufacturing and improve consistency of antibody-drug conjugates.

**Google, Microsoft, and xAI agree to pre-release government AI model evaluations** — 
Google, Microsoft and xAI will share unreleased versions of their AI models with the government to curb cybersecurity threats, the National Institute of Standards and Technology announced on Tuesday. ArXiv, the renowned open academic preprint repository, has recently introduced strict new regulations, announcing a crackdown on the misuse of artificial intelligence in research papers. The core purpose of this move is not to ban the use of technology but to curb the growing problem of some scholars overly relying on AI-generated content without careful verification, leading to a serious decline in the credibility of papers.

## Worth a deeper look

**FDA pilot program for AI-designed drugs signals regulatory framework catching up**

The announcement that the FDA has selected 10 companies for an expedited, interactive review process to evaluate AI-generated evidence in drug submissions marks a critical inflection point for the computational drug discovery sector. The disconnect is stark: over 200 AI-designed drugs are currently in clinical trials worldwide, yet the FDA has not approved a single one. This is not because the drugs are failing—many are progressing through Phase 2 and Phase 3—but because the agency has lacked a clear framework for evaluating computational evidence submitted in lieu of, or alongside, traditional preclinical data.

The pilot program addresses several longstanding uncertainties. First, how should the FDA weigh AI-predicted binding affinities, toxicity profiles, or ADME properties when the models themselves are proprietary black boxes? Second, what standards apply to synthetic training data used to generate drug candidates? Third, how should sponsors document model validation when the models are continuously updated? These questions have been debated at FDA advisory committee meetings, industry conferences, and in the academic literature, but without regulatory clarity, companies have defaulted to conservative approaches—essentially treating AI as an internal tool while submitting traditional preclinical packages.

The selection of 10 companies for the pilot suggests the FDA is moving toward a risk-based, use-case-specific framework rather than a blanket policy. The companies likely span different modalities (small molecules, biologics, gene therapies) and different AI applications (target identification, lead optimization, patient stratification), allowing the agency to develop tailored guidance. This is consistent with the FDA's approach to other novel technologies: digital health tools, for example, were initially regulated through a patchwork of guidance documents before the agency issued comprehensive frameworks.

For the biotech sector, the strategic implication is that companies using AI for drug discovery should prepare for heightened documentation and transparency requirements. The FDA will likely require model performance metrics on external validation sets, disclosure of training data sources and biases, and evidence that AI-generated hypotheses have been experimentally validated at critical decision points. Companies that can demonstrate this level of rigor will have a competitive advantage as the regulatory pathway clarifies. Those that cannot may face delays or rejections, even if their molecules show promise in early trials.
